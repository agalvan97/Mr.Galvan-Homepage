Federalism

The Division of Power Between the National Government and the States

Part 1 · Origins: Why Federalism?

Federalism was not the obvious choice in 1787 — it was a compromise born of failure, fear, and negotiation.

Learning objectives — by the end of this lesson you can:
  • Explain why the Framers replaced the Articles of Confederation with a federal system
  • Classify powers as enumerated, reserved, or concurrent
  • Trace how the federal–state balance shifted across American history
  • Analyze a modern policy conflict (marijuana, healthcare, immigration) through a federalism lens

The failed first attempt: the Articles of Confederation (1781–1789)

America's first constitution created a "league of friendship" — 13 nearly sovereign states with a national government so weak it could barely function. Click each weakness to see its consequence:

No power to tax

Congress could only request money from states. During and after the Revolution the national government went broke, couldn't pay soldiers, and defaulted on war debts. States often simply ignored requisitions.

No power to regulate interstate commerce

States taxed each other's goods and printed their own competing currencies. Trade wars broke out between neighbors like New York and New Jersey — the economy fragmented into 13 pieces.

No executive, no national courts

There was no president to enforce laws and no federal judiciary to settle disputes between states. National laws were essentially suggestions.

Shays' Rebellion (1786–87): the breaking point

Indebted Massachusetts farmers led by Daniel Shays shut down courts and marched on a federal arsenal. Congress had no army and no money to respond — a private militia had to put it down. Elites were terrified: the national government could not even keep basic order. Within months, delegates were on their way to Philadelphia.

The Constitutional Convention: inventing a middle path

The Framers faced two bad models: a unitary system (all power at the center, like the British monarchy they had just escaped) and a confederation (all real power in the states, which had just failed). Their invention — federalism — divided sovereignty itself: two levels of government, each supreme in its own sphere, each acting directly on citizens.

"The powers delegated by the proposed Constitution to the federal government are few and defined. Those which are to remain in the State governments are numerous and indefinite." — James Madison, Federalist No. 45 (1788)
Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists: the ratification fight

Federalists (Madison, Hamilton, Jay) argued a stronger national government was essential for survival, and that dividing power between two levels plus three branches would itself protect liberty — "a double security," Madison called it in Federalist No. 51.

Anti-Federalists (Patrick Henry, George Mason, "Brutus") feared a distant national government would swallow the states and trample individual rights. Their price for ratification: a Bill of Rights — including the 10th Amendment, federalism's constitutional anchor.

Federalism's constitutional hardware

Article I, Section 8 — Enumerated powers + the Elastic Clause

Lists Congress's specific powers (tax, coin money, regulate interstate commerce, declare war...) and ends with the Necessary and Proper Clause, allowing Congress to make all laws "necessary and proper" for carrying out its enumerated powers. This clause becomes the engine of expanding federal power.

Article VI — The Supremacy Clause

The Constitution and federal laws made under it are "the supreme Law of the Land." When a valid federal law and a state law conflict, the federal law wins. Every state judge is bound by it.

The 10th Amendment — Reserved powers

"The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." This is the states' constitutional home base — the source of their police power over health, safety, welfare, and morals.

Article IV — Obligations among states

Full Faith and Credit: states must honor each other's court judgments, records, and licenses. Privileges and Immunities: states can't discriminate against citizens of other states in fundamental matters. Extradition: fugitives must be returned. Federalism requires states to cooperate horizontally, not just vertically with Washington.

Powers denied to everyone

The Constitution also forbids certain powers. Neither level may pass bills of attainder (laws punishing a named person without trial) or ex post facto laws (criminalizing an act after the fact). States additionally may not coin money, enter treaties, or tax imports/exports. The national government may not tax exports or suspend habeas corpus except in rebellion or invasion.

Part 3 · How Federalism Evolved

The federal–state balance has never been fixed. It has swung — through war, depression, and social movements — mostly (but not always) toward Washington.

Two metaphors, two eras

NATIONAL — foreign policy, currency, interstate commerce
STATE — education, police, elections, local commerce
STATE — health, safety, morals ("police power")

Dual federalism (roughly 1789–1930s): national and state governments operate in separate, distinct spheres — like layers of a cake that never mix.

📜 Interactive timeline — click any event to expand

1791Bill of Rights ratified — 10th Amendment anchors state power
The Anti-Federalists' price for ratification. The 10th Amendment reserves undelegated powers to the states or the people, giving states their permanent constitutional foothold.
1819McCulloch v. Maryland — implied powers win
Chief Justice Marshall holds that the Necessary and Proper Clause gives Congress implied powers (like chartering a national bank), and the Supremacy Clause means states cannot tax federal institutions. "The power to tax involves the power to destroy." Federal power's first great expansion.
1824Gibbons v. Ogden — commerce power defined broadly
The Court strikes down a New York steamboat monopoly, reading Congress's power over "commerce among the several states" expansively. The Commerce Clause becomes the workhorse of federal regulation for the next two centuries.
1832Nullification Crisis — can a state veto federal law?
South Carolina declares federal tariffs "null and void" within its borders, invoking states' rights theory. President Jackson threatens force; a compromise tariff defuses it. The underlying question — can states nullify federal law? — smolders until the Civil War answers it: no.
1861–65Civil War — the ultimate federalism conflict
Secession was the most extreme states'-rights claim possible. Union victory settled that the United States is a permanent union, not a compact states can exit. The 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments then imposed national constraints on states — especially the 14th's requirement that states provide due process and equal protection.
191316th & 17th Amendments — money and Senators
The 16th Amendment (federal income tax) gives Washington vast revenue — the fuel for grants that later steer state policy. The 17th (direct election of Senators) removes state legislatures' voice in Congress, weakening states' structural role in the national government.
1930sNew Deal — cooperative federalism arrives
The Great Depression overwhelms the states. FDR's New Deal creates national programs run through states (unemployment insurance, aid to families), funded by federal grants-in-aid. After 1937 the Supreme Court stops striking these down. The layer cake becomes a marble cake — functions mixed at every level.
1964–65Civil Rights era — national power over state resistance
The Civil Rights Act (passed under the Commerce Clause) and Voting Rights Act override Southern states' segregation and disenfranchisement laws. A defining lesson: "states' rights" claims had long shielded racial oppression, and only national power broke it.
1970s–80sNew Federalism — devolution
Presidents Nixon and Reagan push power back toward states: block grants (broad-purpose money with fewer strings) and general revenue sharing replace some narrow categorical grants. The 1996 welfare reform (TANF) is devolution's high-water mark — a federal entitlement converted to state-run block grants.
1995U.S. v. Lopez — the Commerce Clause has limits
For the first time in 60 years, the Court strikes down a federal law (Gun-Free School Zones Act) as exceeding the commerce power. Carrying a gun near a school is not "economic activity." Signals the Rehnquist Court's "federalism revolution" — the pendulum can swing back.
2012NFIB v. Sebelius — the ACA and coercion
The Court upholds the Affordable Care Act's individual mandate as a tax — but rules that forcing states to expand Medicaid by threatening all their existing Medicaid funds is unconstitutionally coercive — "a gun to the head." States get a genuine choice; many initially refuse expansion. A landmark limit on fiscal federalism.

The tools of modern fiscal federalism

Categorical grants — money with strings

Federal funds for a narrow purpose (e.g., highway construction, school lunches) with detailed conditions. The dominant tool since the New Deal. Conditions let Washington shape policy it couldn't command directly — e.g., the 1984 National Minimum Drinking Age Act withheld 5% of highway funds from states that didn't set the drinking age at 21 (upheld in South Dakota v. Dole, 1987).

Block grants — money with flexibility

Broad-purpose funds (e.g., community development, welfare) that states spend with discretion. Favored by advocates of devolution; critics note funding often erodes over time.

Unfunded mandates & preemption

Mandates require state action without paying for it (e.g., ADA accessibility requirements). The 1995 Unfunded Mandates Reform Act curbed but didn't end them. Preemption is when federal law displaces state law entirely in a field (e.g., immigration, airline regulation) via the Supremacy Clause.

Part 5 · Federalism Today

Federalism isn't a history topic — it decides what's legal where you live, right now. For each scenario, think first, then reveal the analysis.

🌿 Marijuana: legal and illegal at the same time

State legalization vs. federal prohibition

Since Colorado and Washington in 2012, most states have legalized marijuana in some form — while it remains a federally controlled substance under the Controlled Substances Act. A state-licensed dispensary is simultaneously legal (state law) and criminal (federal law).

Under the Supremacy Clause, federal law technically prevails — Gonzales v. Raich (2005) even upheld federal power over homegrown medical marijuana. But the anti-commandeering doctrine (Printz v. United States, 1997) means Washington cannot force state police to enforce federal drug law. With limited federal agents and congressional appropriations riders protecting medical marijuana states, enforcement discretion produces a de facto truce. This is federalism as a pressure valve: states act where Congress is gridlocked.

🏥 Healthcare: the Medicaid expansion map

Fiscal federalism and coercion

The ACA offered states generous federal funding to expand Medicaid. After NFIB v. Sebelius made expansion optional, states chose differently — producing a national map where an identical low-income adult is covered in one state and uninsured across the border.

It's both, depending on your federalism theory. Defenders say it's the system working: states as "laboratories of democracy" (Justice Brandeis's phrase) choosing policies matching their voters' preferences. Critics say fundamental welfare shouldn't depend on ZIP code, and only national standards guarantee equality — the same argument that drove the New Deal and civil rights nationalization. The tension between local choice and national equality is federalism's core trade-off.

🛂 Immigration: sanctuary cities vs. federal enforcement

Preemption vs. anti-commandeering

Immigration is a federal power (Arizona v. United States, 2012, struck down most of a state's own immigration enforcement law). But hundreds of "sanctuary" cities and states decline to help federal immigration enforcement — refusing to hold detainees or share information.

Largely yes. Federal supremacy means states can't run their own immigration policy (that's preemption). But anti-commandeering means the federal government can't draft state and local officers into enforcing federal law either. So Washington enforces with its own agents, states decide their own level of cooperation, and both act within their spheres. Note the politics flip: the same doctrines that protect sanctuary cities also protect states that resist federal gun regulations — federalism doctrines are ideologically neutral tools.

🗳️ Elections: 50 states, 50 systems

State administration of federal elections

Even presidential elections are run by states and counties — different ballots, machines, early-voting windows, and ID rules. The Constitution's Elections Clause lets states set the "times, places and manner" of congressional elections, though Congress may override.

Decentralization makes a single nationwide hack or manipulation vastly harder — there is no one system to compromise — and lets states innovate (mail voting, same-day registration). The cost is unequal access and recurring disputes over national standards vs. state control, from the Voting Rights Act of 1965 to today's fights over federal election legislation. Bush v. Gore (2000) showed how state-run elections can still become national crises.

🌎 Climate policy: California as a super-regulator

One state moving a national market

Under the Clean Air Act, California holds a unique waiver to set stricter vehicle-emissions standards than the federal government, and other states may adopt California's rules. Because automakers won't build separate fleets, California often sets de facto national standards — a power that shifts each time a new administration grants or revokes the waiver.

Federalism gives large states market power that mimics regulation. When Washington retreats from a policy area, states with big economies fill the vacuum (the same dynamic appears in data privacy, where California's CCPA became a near-national standard). Critics call it regulation without national representation; supporters call it the laboratories of democracy working at scale.

🦠 COVID-19: a stress test of the system

Public health police power vs. national coordination

Pandemic response exposed federalism's structure in real time: governors — not the president — held the police power to close businesses, mandate masks, and set school policy, while Washington controlled vaccines, borders, and money. Fifty different reopening plans followed.

Both cases are arguable, and strong essays argue each. Help: states tailored responses to wildly different local conditions and citizens could compare results across states. Hurt: viruses don't respect state lines, interstate coordination was weak, and a patchwork response may have cost lives. It's the clearest modern demonstration that the 10th Amendment police power is real and consequential — the federal government legally could not order a national lockdown.

Discussion questions (for class or essay)

  • Madison called federalism a "double security" for liberty. Does it still function that way, or does it mainly produce inequality between states?
  • Pick one issue where you'd favor national uniformity and one where you'd favor state choice. What principle distinguishes them?
  • Both progressives and conservatives have championed "states' rights" at different moments. What does that tell you about federalism as a principle vs. a tactic?
